Most arguments for God start from something large: the beginning of the universe, the order in nature, the moral law. Ibn Sīnā (d. 428/1037) starts from something as small as it can be: _that anything exists at all_. From that one fact he reasons to a being whose existence is <Term translit="wājib al-wujūd">necessary in itself</Term>, owing nothing to anything else.<Sidenote>In his late work _al-Ishārāt wa-l-tanbīhāt_ he calls this route the proof of the truthful, because it reasons from existence itself rather than from any particular thing in the world.</Sidenote>

This essay states the argument in five steps, defends each step, and then sets it beside the argument it is most often confused with.

## The argument in five steps

1. Something exists.
2. Whatever exists is either necessary in itself or <Term translit="imkān">contingent</Term> in itself: either its own nature requires that it exist, or it does not.
3. A contingent thing does not explain its own existence, so it exists through a cause outside itself.
4. If everything that exists were contingent, the whole collection of contingent things would itself be contingent, and so would need a cause outside the collection.
5. A cause outside every contingent thing is not contingent. So something exists that is necessary in itself.

The conclusion is modest on purpose. It does not yet say that the Necessary Existent is one, knowing or willing. Those follow later, from what necessity in itself implies [@marmura2005healing].

## Defending the premises

### Why a contingent thing needs a cause

To call a thing contingent is to say that existing and not existing are equally open to it, as far as its own nature goes. Something must then tip the balance. Ibn Sīnā's point is that _nothing_ cannot tip it: a state of affairs with no cause is a state of affairs with no reason it is this way rather than the other.

### Why the whole chain cannot explain itself

The natural reply is that each contingent thing is explained by an earlier one, without end. Ibn Sīnā grants the chain each link and asks about the chain. Every member of it depends on something else; so does the whole, since a collection exists only if its members do. An infinite chain of dependents is still dependent.<Sidenote>This is why the argument does not need the world to have a beginning. Ibn Sīnā himself held that it had none.</Sidenote>

<PullQuote>An endless line of borrowers never explains where the money came from.</PullQuote>

## What the Qur'an points to

The Qur'an puts the same disjunction as a question, and leaves the hearer to finish it:

## Objections

<Callout type="clarification">
Each objection below gets its own section in the finished essay. They are listed here so the shape of a full post is visible.
</Callout>

- **“Then what caused the Necessary Existent?”** The question assumes it is contingent, which is what the argument denies.[^who]
- **The fallacy of composition.** Hume objected that explaining each member explains the whole. Ibn Sīnā's step 4 is aimed at exactly this.
- **Why call it God?** The step from a Necessary Existent to the God of Islam is the subject of a later part of this course.
