Every tradition that calls itself monotheist must say what it means by _one_. For Muslims the answer is <Term translit="tawḥīd">the doctrine of God's oneness</Term>, the first thing a child learns and the last thing a believer says. The Prophet ﷺ taught it before he taught any law.<Sidenote>Classical scholars often divide tawḥīd into lordship, worship, and names and attributes. The division is a teaching aid, not a scriptural formula.</Sidenote>

This essay reads one short verse beside one famous Christian text, and asks what each is trying to protect.[^asad]

## The Qur'an's statement

The shortest complete statement of the doctrine is the opening of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ:

<Quran
  ref="112:1"
  arabic="قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ"
  translation="Say, “He is Allah, [who is] One.”"
  translator="Sahih International"
/>

The word for one here is _aḥad_ (أحد), not the ordinary numeral _wāḥid_. Commentators note that _aḥad_ excludes not only a second god but any division within God [@nasr2015study].

### Intention and worship

Belief in one God is meant to shape every act. The first report in al-Bukhārī's collection makes the point about intention:

<Hadith
  collection="Sahih al-Bukhari"
  number="1"
  grade="Sahih"
  narrator="Narrated by ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb"
  arabic="إِنَّمَا الأَعْمَالُ بِالنِّيَّاتِ"
>
  Actions are only by intentions.
</Hadith>

Ḥadīth scholarship weighs the chain of narrators, the <Term translit="isnād" />, before it weighs the words [@brown2009hadith].

## The prologue of John

Christian theology reaches its doctrine of God through a different door. The Gospel of John opens:

<Scripture
  tradition="Bible"
  ref="John 1:1"
  version="ESV"
  original="Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος"
  originalLang="grc"
>
  In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.
</Scripture>

<PullQuote>The question is not whether God is one, but what kind of oneness is meant.</PullQuote>

Both texts affirm one God. They differ on whether distinction can exist within that one.[^nicaea]
